There is no single safe time in the sun. For fair skin that usually burns and sometimes tans, an Arizona health department card puts unprotected time near 60 minutes when the UV index is 0 to 2, about 30 minutes at 5 to 6, and about 10 minutes or less above 10. Those figures are a rough burn estimate for one skin description, with no sunscreen. They are not permission to stay out that long.

The US EPA says a "time to burn" should not be read as a period when unprotected sun is risk-free. What sets the clock is today's UV index, your skin type, the hour, the season, altitude, and reflection from snow, water, or sand.

<Callout type="warning" title="A minute count is not a safe window">
  The EPA tells communicators not to present burn times as a stretch when you
  can skip protection. Redness is a late signal. A minimal sunburn is judged by
  reddening that can still be developing about 24 hours later.
</Callout>

## What actually changes the time

Six things move the clock. None of them is a personality trait you can guess from the weather app's temperature.

**UV index.** A higher index means a faster erythemal dose. [WHO](<https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/radiation-the-ultraviolet-(uv)-index>) puts it plainly: the higher the index, the less time it takes for harm to occur.

**Skin type.** Fair skin that burns easily reaches a redness dose sooner than darker skin. Darker skin can still burn.

**Time of day.** UV peaks in the hours around solar noon and drops in the early morning and late afternoon.

**Season.** In the United States, the index is generally higher from spring into summer and lower in winter. Winter does not mean zero.

**Altitude.** Thinner air overhead lets more UV through. Published estimates of how much differ.

**Reflection.** Snow, sand, and water bounce UV back at you. A forecast for open sky misses that extra dose.

## Read the UV index before you read a minute chart

The UV index is a measure of sunburn-weighted UV at the ground. WHO developed it with the UN Environment Programme, the World Meteorological Organization, and the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection. The EPA reports the same scale.

The number you see in a daily forecast is often the day's maximum, in the four-hour period around solar noon. Solar noon falls between noon and 2 p.m. depending on where you are in your time zone and whether daylight saving time is in effect. Morning and late afternoon can be much lower than that single number. Use the hourly forecast for the hour you will actually be outside.

WHO recommends sun protection once the index reaches 3. The EPA's scale page says the same, and adds the shadow rule: a shadow taller than you usually means lower UV, and a shadow shorter than you means you are in the stronger part of the day.

One index unit equals 25 milliwatts per square meter of erythemal dose rate. If the index is about twice as high, and your skin, clothes, and surroundings have not changed, the same redness dose arrives in about half the time. That ratio is the planning rule. It is not a personal limit.

## A published minute range, for one kind of fair skin

The Arizona Department of Health Services prints a sunburn card for fair skin that sometimes tans but usually burns, with no sunscreen. Its labels do not match the EPA scale. On the card, 3 to 4 is called low. On the EPA scale, 3 to 5 is moderate. Use the card for the minutes, and the EPA scale for the words "low" and "high."

## Skin type stretches or compresses the same index

Dermatologists sort sun response with the Fitzpatrick scale, types I through VI. The EPA describes the same idea as the FDA's 1-to-6 skin types: types 1 and 2 burn faster and harder, and types 5 and 6 burn less readily but can still burn.

A 2019 analysis of German UV monitoring used reference minimal erythema doses (MEDs) from the International Commission on Non-Ionizing Radiation Protection. One MED is the dose that produces just-perceptible redness about 24 hours later. One standard erythema dose (SED) is 100 joules per square meter of erythema-weighted UV.

On days when the rounded index was 2, the median shortest time to one of those reference doses was 1.2 hours for type I and 7.35 hours for type VI. That is a low-UV day in Germany, not a beach afternoon at index 9. It shows the spread. It does not give you a summer timetable.

The type VI line on that scale is a description, not a guarantee. The EPA is explicit that darker skin can burn. On UV 2 days in that German record, two hours around noon passed the type I reference dose on nearly 88% of days and the type II dose on 69% of days. "Low" on the forecast is slower. It is not an all-afternoon pass for the fairest skin.

The Arizona card's 60 minutes covers the whole 0-to-2 band for one fair-skin description. These medians are measured times to a reference dose on UV 2 days only. Fair skin lands in the same neighborhood. The calculations are not the same, so do not average them into a personal number.

You cannot assign your type from a photo of your forearm. If you burn easily, have many moles, or have had a skin cancer, a dermatologist can help you judge your risk. This page cannot.

## Time of day and season

The CDC says UVB is strongest from about 10 a.m. to 4 p.m., and stronger in summer, at higher elevation, and closer to the equator. The Skin Cancer Foundation describes UVB as the band mainly responsible for sunburn. UVA stays high through more of the daylight hours. Heat is a bad proxy for either one. You can burn when the air feels mild.

EPA's UV Index guide shows how far a typical midday value can swing from winter to summer. These are examples from the guide, not a live forecast. The guide says clouds and ozone move them.

Clouds do not give you a free pass. The EPA guide says heavy cloud can block most UV, thin or broken cloud lets most of it through, and some fair-weather clouds can raise the UV that reaches you. The American Academy of Dermatology notes that on cloudy days, up to 80% of the sun's UV can still get through.

## Altitude, snow, water, and sand

The forecast is calculated for a set of sky conditions. The ground around you can add UV the number does not show.

The EPA guide says snow may reflect as much as 80% of UV, sand about 15%, and water about 10%. The Skin Cancer Foundation uses the same "up to 80%" figure for snow, which is why a bright ski day burns faces, ears, and the skin under the chin. The American Academy of Dermatology tells people to take extra care near water, snow, and sand for the same reason. The CDC notes that reflection off snow can nearly double the UVB reaching the eyes, which is the setup for snow blindness.

Altitude stacks on top of that. The EPA guide estimates about a 2% increase in UV for every 1,000 feet of elevation. The Skin Cancer Foundation puts the increase at 4 to 5% per 1,000 feet. The direction is not in dispute. The percent is. A slope at 8,000 feet is a stronger dose than the valley forecast you checked at breakfast, and neither figure is precise enough to convert into extra minutes for you.

## How to use the UV index to plan your time

Check the hourly index for the spot you will stand in. A citywide daily maximum tells you the worst hour, not your hour.

Match the band to the action in the table above. From 3 up, build the outing around shade and clothing, then sunscreen on what is still bare. At 8 and above, move the outing away from the middle of the day if you can.

Scale the duration with the index. A sun timer that ignores today's number will run long on a UV 9 afternoon and short on a UV 3 morning. If you know you redden in about half an hour on a UV 6 day at home, a day near UV 10 is a shorter outing, because the dose rate tracks the index. That is a proportion, not a prescription.

Use sunscreen as directed, and do not spend the protection on a longer session. The American Academy of Dermatology says a higher SPF does not let you stay out longer without reapplying, and to reapply about every two hours, or after swimming or sweating. No sunscreen is waterproof. The EPA says doctors caution against using sunscreen to prolong time in the sun.

Leave a margin for snow, water, sand, altitude, thin cloud, and any medicine that makes you sun-sensitive. Go in on the clock. Do not wait until you look pink.

## The vitamin D hours are often the burn hours

Skin can make vitamin D when the UV index is high enough, generally from about 3 up. Those are the same hours burn risk climbs. A short outing inside your [vitamin D sun window](/blog/vitamin-d-sun-window) is a different job from staying until you redden. The [UV index cutoff for vitamin D](/blog/what-uv-index-do-you-need-for-vitamin-d) is the production side of that line.

Winter can split the two problems apart. In many northern places the vitamin D window closes for weeks, which is the subject of [vitamin D from winter sun](/blog/vitamin-d-in-winter). A ski day in that same season can still burn you, because snow and altitude add UV while the air feels cold. Weak vitamin D sun is not the same thing as harmless sun.

## A tan does not buy you reliable time

A [base tan](/blog/does-a-base-tan-protect-from-sunburn) is a sign your skin has already taken UV damage. It is not a layer of protection you can add to the minute chart. Higher UV tans faster and burns faster. If color is part of why you are outside, the [UV index for tanning](/blog/best-uv-index-for-tanning) is about that tradeoff. It is not a reason to stay through the burn.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is there a safe number of minutes in the sun?**

No single number fits everyone. The EPA says not to treat a time-to-burn figure as a risk-free window. Use the UV index, your skin, shade, and clothing. A personal limit is a question for a dermatologist, especially if you burn easily, have had a skin cancer, or take a medicine that makes you more sensitive to light.

**Can you burn when it is cloudy or cold?**

Yes. Heavy cloud can block a lot of UV, and thin cloud often does not. The American Academy of Dermatology says up to 80% of UV can pass through clouds. Cold air does not turn the index off, and snow can reflect as much as 80% of UV.

**Does darker skin mean you will not burn?**

No. Darker skin reaches a redness dose more slowly. The EPA notes that the darkest skin types can still burn. On low UV days in the German monitoring study, the gap between the fairest and darkest types was hours, not a promise of zero risk.

**Does a higher SPF let me stay out longer?**

It blocks more UVB when you apply enough of it. It does not change the reapplication clock. The American Academy of Dermatology says high-SPF and low-SPF sunscreens last the same amount of time, and that a higher SPF does not allow extra time outside without reapplying. Reapply about every two hours, and after swimming or sweating.

**Why do I burn faster at the beach or on a ski slope than the forecast suggests?**

The forecast does not include every reflection, and it may be for a lower elevation than the slope. Sand, water, and snow add UV. Altitude adds more. Use a shorter plan than the open-sky number suggests, and cover the skin the reflection hits from below.
