Your vitamin D sun window is the part of today when the UV where you are is strong enough for your skin to make vitamin D. It usually sits around the middle of the day. As the days get shorter, that stretch of useful sun exposure gets narrower, and on some days it never opens. Check today's hours, then put a short outing inside them.

## Day length and your vitamin D window run on different clocks

Daylight shrinks after the summer solstice. Vitamin D production does not track sunrise and sunset. UVB has to reach 7-dehydrocholesterol in your skin, and that slice of sunlight fades first when the sun is low. A bright morning and a bright late afternoon can both outlast the hours that still count.

The [NIH Office of Dietary Supplements](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/VitaminD-HealthProfessional/) lists what changes the yield: season, time of day, length of day, cloud cover, smog, skin melanin, and sunscreen. Older adults and people with darker skin make less vitamin D from the same exposure.

The geometry is in [the angle of the sun and vitamin D](/blog/vitamin-d-angle-of-sun). Midday versus morning and evening is in [the best time of day to get vitamin D](/blog/best-time-of-day-to-get-vitamin-d). This page is about the weeks when a window still exists and is short enough to miss.

## Sun exposure only counts inside today's window

The window is local. The [US EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sunsafety/learn-about-uv-index) describes the UV Index as a forecast of overexposure risk. The National Weather Service calculates it for most US ZIP codes from forecast ozone, clouds, elevation, and the sun's angle, which depends on latitude, date, and time of day. Two cities on the same October afternoon do not share a window, and tomorrow's clouds can close a day that looked fine on the calendar.

Plan around a UV index of at least 3. Under that, extra minutes do very little for vitamin D. The [UV index guide](/blog/what-uv-index-do-you-need-for-vitamin-d) explains the cutoff. The EPA uses 3 for a different decision: on its scale, which follows World Health Organization reporting, 1-2 is low and 3-7 is moderate to high. From 3 up, it says to seek shade from late morning through mid-afternoon and to use clothing, a hat, sunglasses, and broad-spectrum SPF 15 or higher. Keep your time in those hours short.

Outside, the [EPA shadow rule](https://www.epa.gov/sunsafety/uv-index-scale-0) is a rough check. A shadow taller than you, early or late, usually means lower UV. A shorter shadow, around midday, means higher UV. The forecast is what tells you to hold the hour before something else takes it.

[Cancer Council Australia](https://www.cancer.org.au/cancer-information/causes-and-prevention/sun-safety/vitamin-d) describes the wide-window case: when the UV Index is 3 or above, most people there maintain adequate vitamin D with a few minutes outdoors on most days, and protection is recommended at that level. In some southern Australian areas, when the index stays under 3 in late autumn and winter, they still suggest midday time with some skin uncovered, because midday is the least-weak hour. That is a thinner chance than a summer window, and it is local advice. If your forecast peaks under 3, a longer session will not make the day up. Months when winter sun fails by latitude are covered in [vitamin D in winter](/blog/vitamin-d-in-winter).

## How a fall noon gets less casual

A 2024 paper by Kift and Webb estimated clear-sky minutes at noon to maintain vitamin D status: white skin, about 35% of the skin exposed (shorts and a T-shirt), standing and moving, averaged by latitude. "Maintain" means hold the blood marker steady, not correct a low level. These are model estimates for ideal skies, not a personal timer. The cities below sit near those latitudes. They were not measured in the study.

At 30°N the clear-sky noon estimate barely moves from June into October. At 40°N it goes from 4 minutes in June to 8 in October and 15 in November, and the model marks December as a vitamin D winter. At 50°N, October is already 15 minutes and November has no maintenance dose from the sun on that map. For skin type V, the paper scales exposure time by about 2.5 compared with white skin, so those months stop being a quick step outside.

Clouds are missing from the table on purpose. Engelsen and colleagues modeled how many hours per day UV stays above a synthesis threshold. Clouds, aerosols, and a thick ozone layer cut that duration sharply, and heavy cloud can shut synthesis down even at the equator. A month can look open while today is not. [Cloudy days](/blog/can-you-get-vitamin-d-on-a-cloudy-day) move the plan more than the calendar does. Other models also close the season on different dates, which is why today's UV index beats a month label. The measured winter pattern is in the [winter article](/blog/vitamin-d-in-winter).

## Put today's window on the calendar

**Check the forecast for the place you will actually be.** A UV index for your ZIP code, or the local equivalent outside the United States, beats a seasonal rule of thumb. If you can see tomorrow as well as today, you can move a walk before the slot is gone.

**Mark the hours at or above 3.** That span is the candidate window. The strong part is the middle, near solar noon, which is often not 12:00 on the clock. [Solar noon](/blog/best-time-of-day-to-get-vitamin-d) shifts with where you sit in your time zone and with daylight saving time.

**Match the plan to the width.** A wide summer band forgives a late start. A short fall band does not. If the index only clears 3 for a slice around the middle of the day, pick that slice the day before: lunch outside, a lap around the block between calls, the walk to the train if the timing lands inside the hours.

**Keep it short, on skin you already uncover.** The NIH notes that some expert groups suggest about 5 to 30 minutes between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m., on the face, arms, hands, and legs, without sunscreen, daily or at least twice a week. The same fact sheet says UV, skin, and behavior make one sun rule unreliable. [UK NICE guidance](https://www.nice.org.uk/guidance/ng34/chapter/supporting-information-for-practitioners), for Britain from March to October, puts the strongest sun between 11 a.m. and 3 p.m. Short periods with forearms, hands, or lower legs uncovered are what they describe for most people, longer for darker skin. Before 11 a.m. and after 3 p.m., the same exposure takes longer, and burning or tanning is unlikely to add more vitamin D. UV is a carcinogen, and the EPA recommends protection once the index is 3 or higher. How sunscreen fits that is in [does sunscreen block vitamin D](/blog/does-sunscreen-block-vitamin-d).

**Recheck the week the clocks change.** If your clocks fall back an hour, solar noon arrives an hour earlier by the clock. A 1 p.m. habit from September can land outside a narrow late-fall window.

Morning light still matters for your body clock, which is a different job, so an 8 a.m. walk can feel good and miss this window. [Morning sunlight](/blog/morning-sunlight-benefits) covers that split. Sitting indoors behind glass misses it too. The NIH notes that UVB does not pass through glass. See [vitamin D through a window](/blog/can-you-get-vitamin-d-through-a-window).

## When the forecast never clears the line

Some days the index stays under 3 from sunrise to sunset. More time outside does not open a window the UV never reaches. Engelsen's group treated production as something that starts only after UV passes a threshold, and the hours above it can drop to zero. Thick cloud can do that in a month that looked fine on a clear-sky chart. Latitude can do it for weeks.

Notice the miss. Food and supplements are the other inputs when sun exposure cannot cover you. [Vitamin D from sun vs. supplements](/blog/vitamin-d-from-sun-vs-supplements) compares them. Whether you need a supplement is a question for a clinician, ideally with a blood test for 25-hydroxyvitamin D.

  enough that you do not burn.
</Callout>

## Frequently asked questions

**How do I find my vitamin D sun window today?**

Look up the UV index forecast for where you will be. The window is the stretch when the index is at least 3, usually around the middle of the day. Under that line, staying out longer does not replace the hours you missed.

**Do shorter days mean I need more sun exposure?**

They mean fewer useful hours, nearer the middle of the day. Inside those hours, clear-sky noon estimates creep up through fall, especially farther from the equator. Extra time early or late does not replace a missed window, and the NIH says no single sun rule fits everyone.

**What if I am indoors through the middle of the day?**

Use the edges only when the forecast says they are still high enough: a few minutes at lunch, or just before the window closes. If the whole window falls during a shift you cannot leave, that day's sun exposure is not available. Diet and supplements are the other routes, and a clinician can help you decide what you need.

**Does a bright, cool afternoon count?**

Only if the UV index says so. Cloud can remove the window on a bright day, and cool air does not mean weak UV. NICE notes that a burn is still possible when it is cool or cloudy, including at midday in summer and at other times of year. Check the index for your location.

**I live in the Southern Hemisphere. When does this happen?**

The same pattern, about half a year later. Shorter days and a tighter vitamin D window show up around June, not December. The daily move is the same: use today's UV where you are.
